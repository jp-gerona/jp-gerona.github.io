# Editorial principles for personal technical writing

Read when revisiting the skill's rationale, not before every draft. These are editorial recommendations synthesized from writing research and the author's stated preferences. They are not observations of approved prose by the author or a model of another author's personality.

## Sentence construction

Let longer sentences carry related dependencies, qualifications, or sequences. Separate an important consequence when readers need a pause. Do not alternate sentence lengths mechanically. Use contractions where natural, and place uncertainty beside the claim it limits rather than weakening every statement.

Parentheses can hold optional context or a small aside. Keep essential technical constraints in the main explanation. Follow repository punctuation rules without replacing one punctuation habit with another.

## Paragraph construction

Begin with whatever advances the thought: a circumstance, a limitation, an example, or the next step in an explanation. Develop one local idea across as many sentences as it needs. A paragraph break can distinguish explanation from judgment without fragmenting every sentence.

Carry a concrete noun, constraint, or consequence between paragraphs. Explain what code changes mean to the reader. Link optional details when they would interrupt the main account. Keep introductions and conclusions only when they add something useful.

## Narrative structure

A situation, attempted response, and consequence can explain a project, but their order depends on the material. A technical article may need a mechanism first; an update may start with the result. These relationships are possibilities, not mandatory headings.

Keep reflections grounded in supplied events or decisions. An experiment may end in an imperfect state or an unanswered question. Do not invent frustration, a breakthrough, or a lesson to complete a story.

## Voice and personality

Make preferences inspectable through the example or constraint behind them. Readers should be able to understand the reasoning even when they disagree. Preserve genuine enthusiasm, skepticism, or self-awareness when the author supplies it. Do not manufacture a persona to make sparse facts feel personal.

Use the author's own details and perspective. Another author's biography, signature language, technical preferences, and mannerisms are not evidence of his voice. Approved personal samples take priority over these recommendations.

## Density and clarity

Introduce a technical concept when readers need it to follow the argument. Familiar tool names need no automatic lecture. Prefer a worked example when it reveals a constraint more clearly than an abstract claim.

Give longer explanations room for code and detail. In short copy, retain details that change the reader's understanding of the work. Concision should not erase the specific observation that makes an account worth reading.

## Editorial limits

These principles do not prescribe sentence counts, paragraph lengths, or a fixed narrative pattern. Every project need not begin with frustration; every paragraph need not ask a question; every ending need not offer a lesson. A natural draft can remain unchanged.

Judge choices against the audience, the available facts, and the author's feedback. The goal is to make his reasoning easier to follow while preserving its character.
